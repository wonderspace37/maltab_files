## Is it legal to rent out just one room?

Yes. In California it is legal for a homeowner to rent a single room in their
own residence. How the law treats the arrangement depends on the setup:

- **Lodger (most common for this situation).** California Civil Code
  **§ 1946.5** covers the case where a homeowner rents a room to **one**
  lodger in an owner-occupied home, with no other lodgers. The lodger has
  access to some common areas (kitchen, bathroom, etc.) and the owner keeps
  overall control of the house. A single lodger can be removed with
  **written notice equal to the rental period** (e.g., 30 days for
  month-to-month) — a formal unlawful detainer (eviction) lawsuit is not
  required in that narrow single-lodger case.
- **Tenant.** If there are **two or more** lodgers, or the room is essentially
  a self-contained unit, normal California landlord-tenant law applies —
  including the formal eviction process and most of the statewide tenant
  protections (AB 1482 rent cap/just-cause rules may also apply depending on
  the property type and age).
- **Fremont-specific.** Fremont does not have its own rent-control ordinance,
  but Alameda County and state laws (AB 1482, Fair Employment and Housing
  Act, etc.) apply. The city's Rent Review Program can mediate rent
  disputes.
- **Age.** You are 18, so you can legally sign a binding contract in
  California.
- **Security deposit cap (CA Civil Code § 1950.5).** For an unfurnished room,
  the deposit is capped at **one month's rent** (two months' if the owner has
  more than two residential units anywhere). Must be returned, with an
  itemized statement, within **21 days** of move-out.
- **Fair housing / discrimination.** Most fair-housing rules still apply, but
  an owner-occupied home renting to a single roomer has limited exemptions
  under the federal "Mrs. Murphy" rule and California's Unruh Act.
- **Household impact.** If the homeowner has a mortgage, HOA, or receives
  certain benefits (Section 8, property-tax exemptions, etc.), renting out a
  room can affect those — worth a quick check before you sign.

#### 3a. Late Payment Penalty

If any term's base rent is not paid in full by its Rent Due Date, a
**late penalty of $21.25** (approximately 3.62% of the $587.50 base
rent, or roughly one day's rent) is added to that term's balance
**for each "Period Late,"** calculated as follows:

- **1 day or more past the Rent Due Date = 1 Period Late** (adds
  **$21.25**).
- The "Period Late" count **increases by 1 each time another full
  six-month (182-day) period elapses** without the rent being paid in
  full.
  - 1–182 days past due  → **1 Period Late**  → **+$21.25**
  - 183–364 days past due → **2 Periods Late** → **+$42.50**
  - 365–546 days past due → **3 Periods Late** → **+$63.75**
  - 547–728 days past due → **4 Periods Late** → **+$85.00**
  - 729–910 days past due → **5 Periods Late** → **+$106.25**
  - 911–1092 days past due → **6 Periods Late** → **+$127.50**
  - 1093–1274 days past due → **7 Periods Late** → **+$148.75**
- The penalty is **simple** (not compounded): each additional Period
  Late adds another flat $21.25 to the balance owed on top of the
  unpaid $587.50 base rent.
- Once a term's rent and all accrued penalties are paid in full, the
  Period-Late counter for that term stops.

> **Enforceability note.** Under California law (Civil Code § 1671 and
> related case law), a residential late fee must be a **reasonable
> estimate of the actual damage** the late payment causes the landlord —
> not a punitive penalty. A $21.25-per-six-months escalating fee on
> $587.50 base rent (~3.62% per period, roughly equal to one day's
> rent) is a modest figure and is well within the range California
> courts routinely uphold as a good-faith liquidated-damages estimate;
> the risk of it being struck as an unenforceable penalty is low. The
> Parties nonetheless agree that if a court finds any figure above
> unenforceable, the late fee will be reduced to the maximum the court
> will enforce, and the rest of this Agreement still applies.

   with at least **24 hours' prior notice** given in person, by text, or

belongings.

### 9. Maintenance and Repairs

- The Homeowner keeps the home in a habitable condition (working
  plumbing, heat, electricity, locks, smoke detectors, etc.).
- The Renter will promptly report any damage, leaks, or safety issues
  in writing (text or email is fine).
- The Renter pays for damage caused by the Renter or the Renter's
  guests beyond normal wear and tear.

### 10. Alterations

The Renter will not paint, drill, install new locks, or make other
permanent changes to the Room or home without the Homeowner's written
consent. Command-strip hooks and similar non-damaging fixtures are OK.

### 11. Insurance

- The Homeowner maintains homeowner's insurance on the structure.
- The Homeowner's insurance does **not** cover the Renter's personal
  belongings. The Renter is encouraged to obtain **renter's insurance**
  (often ~$10–$20/month).

### 12. Termination

- **By either party (month-to-month):** at least **30 days' written
  notice**.
- **By the Homeowner, for cause** (non-payment, safety risk, repeated
  rule violations after written warning): notice as required by
  California Civil Code § 1946.5 — written notice equal to the length
  of the rental-payment period (typically 30 days).
- On termination, the Renter will remove all personal belongings,
  return keys, and leave the Room in the condition received, reasonable
  wear and tear excepted.
- The single-lodger rule of Civil Code § 1946.5 only works while the
  Homeowner is the sole non-lodger occupant and the Renter is the only
  lodger in the home. If that changes, normal tenant eviction rules
  apply.

### 13. Entire Agreement; Changes

This Agreement is the entire agreement between the Parties about the
Room. Any change must be in writing and signed by both Parties. If any
part of this Agreement is unenforceable, the rest still applies.

### 14. Governing Law

California law governs this Agreement. Venue for any dispute is Alameda
County, California. The Parties will first try to resolve disputes by
informal conversation, then mediation (e.g., Fremont Rent Review
Program) before going to court.
